\> \*\*Purpose\*\*

\>

\> Establish the constitutional governance framework governing
artificial intelligence participation within engineering activities.
This standard defines authority, trust, evidence requirements, promotion
rules, and human oversight for AI-assisted development across the
Knowledge Infrastructure Bootstrap Kit, Sovereign OS, the Digital
Archive, and associated engineering environments.

---

\# Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is capable of accelerating engineering
activities through software development, documentation, analysis,
testing, and technical review.

However, increasing capability does not imply increasing authority.

This standard establishes that artificial intelligence functions as an
engineering collaborator operating under governance rather than as an
autonomous decision maker.

Engineering authority remains vested in governed human review supported
by evidence, reproducibility, and documented approval.

---

\# Mission

Enable productive collaboration between human engineers and artificial
intelligence while preserving:

\- deterministic engineering;

\- reproducibility;

\- governance;

\- provenance;

\- software quality;

\- operational accountability.

---

\# Scope

This standard governs:

\- AI-assisted software engineering;

\- documentation generation;

\- engineering review;

\- automated testing;

\- repository modifications;

\- promotion between trust levels;

\- evidence generation;

\- approval workflows.

This document does not define software installation procedures or
project-specific workflows.

\# Foundational Principles

\## Governance Before Automation

Governance defines authority.

Automation performs work within that authority.

Automation shall never redefine governance.

---

\## Human Responsibility

Artificial intelligence may assist engineering.

Responsibility remains with human reviewers.

Authority cannot be delegated to automated systems.

---

\## Learn Before Automating

Developer understanding remains a primary objective.

Automation should accelerate learning rather than replace it.

---

\## Evidence Before Promotion

No engineering artefact progresses toward production without sufficient
supporting evidence.

Evidence is generated through testing, review, verification, and
documentation.

---

\## Reproducibility

Engineering outcomes should be reproducible by independent reviewers.

All significant modifications should be supported by:

\- documented reasoning;

\- repository history;

\- verification evidence;

\- reproducible testing.

---

\# Trust Model

Every engineering artefact exists within a defined trust level.

Higher trust levels require increasingly rigorous verification.

Trust is earned through evidence rather than generated by the source of
the work.

---

\# Trust Levels

\## Level 0 — Proposal

Characteristics

\- brainstorming

\- AI generated

\- incomplete

\- exploratory

\- no authority

Typical artefacts

\- draft code

\- architecture ideas

\- design notes

\- proposed implementations

Promotion Requirements

Human review required.

---

\## Level 1 — Verified

Characteristics

\- compiles successfully

\- testing completed

\- documented evidence

\- reproducible build

Typical artefacts

\- tested implementation

\- successful compilation

\- passing validation

Promotion Requirements

Engineering review required.

---

\## Level 2 — Approved

Characteristics

\- architecture reviewed

\- governance compliant

\- accepted by maintainer

\- repository ready

Promotion Requirements

Formal approval.

---

\## Level 3 — Canonical

Characteristics

\- merged

\- version controlled

\- archived

\- authoritative baseline

Canonical artefacts represent official project history.

\# Human-in-the-Loop Governance

Human review represents the final authority for engineering promotion.

AI systems may:

\- propose;

\- analyse;

\- explain;

\- implement;

\- review;

\- document.

Humans remain responsible for:

\- approval;

\- governance;

\- publication;

\- architectural integrity;

\- project direction.

---

\# Engineering Authority

Authority remains explicitly separated from capability.

Possessing the capability to generate code does not confer authority to
publish it.

Engineering authority requires:

\- governance;

\- evidence;

\- review;

\- accountability.

---

\# AI Responsibilities

Artificial intelligence should primarily contribute through:

\- implementation assistance;

\- documentation;

\- testing support;

\- static analysis;

\- code explanation;

\- architecture discussion;

\- issue investigation;

\- proposal generation.

Outputs remain recommendations until promoted through governance.

---

\# Human Responsibilities

Human engineers remain responsible for:

\- defining objectives;

\- reviewing proposals;

\- validating evidence;

\- approving promotions;

\- accepting risk;

\- maintaining governance;

\- preserving architectural integrity.

---

\# Evidence Requirements

Every significant engineering change should be accompanied by evidence
appropriate to its scope.

Evidence may include:

\- compilation results;

\- unit tests;

\- integration tests;

\- benchmark results;

\- architecture notes;

\- code review;

\- documentation updates;

\- issue references.

---

\# Provenance

Every promoted engineering artefact should preserve sufficient
provenance to reconstruct:

\- origin;

\- reasoning;

\- implementation history;

\- review history;

\- approval history.

---

\# Branch Governance

AI-generated work should remain isolated until approved.

Recommended progression:

Experimental Branch

↓

Review Branch

↓

Protected Main Branch

Direct modification of protected branches by autonomous systems is
prohibited.

---

\# Separation of Capability and Authority

Capability answers:

"What can the system do?"

Authority answers:

"What is the system permitted to do?"

Capability may expand as technology evolves.

Authority remains governed.

---

\# Constitutional Rule

No autonomous subsystem shall possess unilateral authority to promote
changes into a higher-trust state.

Promotion requires:

\- successful verification;

\- evidence generation;

\- policy evaluation;

\- human approval;

\- provenance recording.

Automation prepares work.

Governance authorises progression.

---

\# Review Requirements

Every promotion should answer:

\- Does it compile?

\- Does it pass tests?

\- Is evidence complete?

\- Does it comply with governance?

\- Has architecture been preserved?

\- Has a human approved promotion?

If any answer is negative, promotion should stop.

---

\# Operational Outcomes

Successful implementation of this standard should result in:

\- improved engineering productivity;

\- reduced repetitive work;

\- preserved software quality;

\- deterministic governance;

\- complete auditability;

\- stronger developer understanding;

\- maintainable repositories.

---

\# Future Evolution

As AI capability increases, this standard should remain stable.

Future technologies—including autonomous agents, multi-agent
collaboration, advanced reasoning systems, or future Sovereign OS
orchestration—remain subordinate to these governance principles.

Increasing capability should increase the quality of engineering
assistance rather than reduce governance.

Artificial intelligence is an engineering capability—not an engineering
authority.

accountable human review.

Automation may accelerate engineering.

Governance determines what becomes trusted.
